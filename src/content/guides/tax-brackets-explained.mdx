## What Are Tax Brackets?

Tax brackets are the divisions that define how much of your income is taxed at each rate. In a progressive tax system, your income is "bracketed" — different portions are taxed at different rates.

## How Brackets Are Structured

### Marginal Rate vs. Effective Rate

Two key concepts often get confused:

- **Marginal Tax Rate**: The rate applied to your last dollar of income. If you earn $85,000 and the top bracket you fall into is 22%, then your marginal rate is 22%.
- **Effective Tax Rate**: The actual percentage of your total income that goes to taxes. It's your total tax divided by total income.

<Tip>
  Your marginal rate is NOT your overall tax rate. Most people's effective rate is significantly lower than their top marginal bracket.
</Tip>

## Understanding Bracket Thresholds

Tax brackets are defined by **income thresholds** (also called bracket boundaries). These thresholds are adjusted periodically for inflation to prevent "bracket creep" — where inflation pushes taxpayers into higher brackets even though their real income hasn't changed.

### Example: A Three-Bracket System

| Annual Taxable Income | Rate |
|---|---|
| $0 – $50,000 | 10% |
| $50,001 – $100,000 | 20% |
| Above $100,000 | 30% |

In this example:
- The first $50,000 is taxed at 10%
- Income between $50,001 and $100,000 is taxed at 20%
- Income above $100,000 is taxed at 30%

## Common Bracket Mistakes

### Thinking all income is taxed at your top rate

This is the most common bracket misunderstanding. Only income above the threshold is taxed at the higher rate. Income in lower brackets is still taxed at those lower rates.

### Ignoring bracket adjustments

Many tax systems adjust bracket thresholds annually for inflation. Failing to account for this can lead to incorrect estimates.

### Confusing gross income with taxable income

Brackets apply to **taxable income**, not gross income. Deductions and exemptions reduce your taxable income and can keep you in a lower bracket.

## How to Find Your Bracket

1. Calculate your **total deductions** (standard or itemized).
2. Subtract deductions from your gross income to get **taxable income**.
3. Look up the taxable income thresholds for your filing status.
4. Determine which bracket your taxable income falls into.

## Practical Tips

- Use a tax calculator to estimate your bracket accurately.
- Consider how contributions to retirement accounts or health Savings Accounts (HSAs) can reduce taxable income and potentially drop you into a lower bracket.
- Review your withholding during the year to avoid surprises at tax time.

## Summary

Tax brackets define which portions of your income are taxed at which rates. Understanding them helps you make informed decisions about deductions, contributions, and income planning.
